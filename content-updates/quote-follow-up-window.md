## When the Estimate Goes Out, the Real Race Begins

You sent the estimate at 2:14 p.m. The customer opened it at 2:47 p.m. Then nothing. Three days later, they went with the next contractor on their list. Same scope, same price, faster follow-up.

That is the most common reason service businesses lose jobs they have already quoted. Not pricing. Not scope. The estimate sat in an inbox for two days while the customer had new questions, second thoughts, and three other bids to compare.

The companies that win it are not the ones with the best numbers. They are the ones who show up again, in the customer's preferred channel, before the silence does.

## The Math Most Owners Do Not Run

If you sent ten estimates last month and closed three, you did not lose seven to bad pricing. You lost seven to drift. The customer had a question on day one no one answered. They got a competing quote on day two. They called a friend on day three who recommended someone else.

A typical service business sees 60 to 80 percent of closed jobs come from estimates sent in the last fourteen days. The estimate itself does not lose the deal. The silence after it does.

The good news is that this is the easiest part of the sales cycle to fix. You already did the hard work. What is missing is a small, predictable sequence of touches that keeps the conversation warm.

## What Goes Wrong in the Quote Gap

Most owners treat the estimate as the finish line. They send it, wait, and check the calendar at the end of the week. In reality, three things usually happen in between.

The customer has a new question they did not think of until after reading the estimate. If no one answers within a few hours, the customer fills the gap with their own assumption.

The customer has a competing bid in hand. Whoever calls them back first with a refinement wins attention.

The customer's urgency fades. A kid gets sick, a budget question comes up, a spouse wants to compare. Every day without a touch from you is a day for urgency to fade into someday. That is where most estimates go to die.

A short follow-up sequence prevents all three.

## The Four-Message Quote Follow-Up Sequence

You need four short messages, on a predictable cadence, in the channel the customer uses. Email works for some. Text works for most. Pick one and stick to it.

### Message 1. Same day, two to four hours after sending the estimate

A short note confirming you sent it and offering to answer questions. Example: "Hi [name], this is [business]. Just sent your estimate for [project]. Happy to walk through any part of it. Just reply here."

Most estimates that go cold never get a single reply from the business. This fixes that.

### Message 2. Day two, morning

A second touch with one useful detail. Example: "Hi [name], quick follow-up. Wanted to mention [one relevant detail: a recent similar job, current lead time, an option to phase the work]."

Pick one detail. Do not turn the message into another estimate.

### Message 3. Day four or five

A direct check-in on the customer's timeline. Example: "Hi [name], checking in. Still planning to move forward, or has anything changed? Happy to revise scope or timing."

This is where most owners get nervous. It feels pushy. It is not. Customers expect a check-in.

### Message 4. Day ten or fourteen

A clean close that gives the customer a way to come back later. Example: "Hi [name], closing the loop. We are booked out [X] weeks. If you want to move forward, reply and we will hold a slot. The estimate stands for sixty days."

This creates gentle urgency without a fake countdown and leaves the door open.

## What to Put in Your System Right Now

Add three columns to your spreadsheet or CRM: estimate sent date, last customer reply, next follow-up due. When the next follow-up is due, send it. When the customer replies, reset the counter. Nothing in this sequence should depend on you remembering. Memory is the single most common reason estimates go cold.

If you have a CRM, set the four touchpoints as a workflow triggered by sending the estimate. The setup is an afternoon. The payoff starts the same week.

## Why Owners Do Not Send the Fourth Message

Most owners send message one. Some send message two. Almost no one sends three or four. The reasons: fear of seeming pushy, busy on a job and forgetting, or assuming silence means the customer is not interested.

None of those survive contact with the data. The customers who close are the ones who got touched three or four times. The follow-up is not the problem. The absence of follow-up is.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the best channel for quote follow-up?

Match the channel the customer used to first reach you. If they texted, follow up by text. If they emailed, follow up by email. If they called, call again and send a text or email summary.

### How many follow-ups is too many?

Four short touches over two weeks. More than that and the customer feels pushed. Fewer than that and the estimate usually goes cold.

### What if the customer says they need to think about it?

Reply with a question. Ask what part they want to think through, or whether a small adjustment would help. Most replies convert when you keep the conversation easy to continue.

### Should I discount the estimate to win the deal?

Discounting is the most expensive mistake in estimate follow-up. The customer rarely asked for a lower price. They asked for confidence. A small scope adjustment or a clear timeline usually does more than a 10 percent cut.

### What if the customer never replies?

Send the fourth message and move on. Add them to a reactivation list and check in sixty to ninety days later. A meaningful share of lost estimates come back when the customer's timeline changes.
